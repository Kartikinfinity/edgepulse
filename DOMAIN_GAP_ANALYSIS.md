# Why the baseline fails on real audio — measured

**Date:** 2026-09-10 · **Tool:** `tools/domain_analysis.py` · **Model under test:** model-1 (EXP-006, frozen in `artifacts/model_1_baseline/`)

EXP-006 established the failure and rejected level and peak-normalisation as
causes. "Domain gap" was the remaining label. This document turns that label
into numbers, so the data strategy that follows is chosen on evidence rather
than on the assumption that more recordings must help.

---

## 1. The question that decides everything

**Can any signal-processing change close the gap, or is real training audio
required?**

Method: fit a linear classifier to tell real INMP441 positives from synthetic
TTS positives, using the clip features the model consumes (per-coefficient
mean, standard deviation and mean absolute delta). 5-fold cross-validated
accuracy. **0.50 means the two domains are indistinguishable; 1.00 means they
are trivially separable.**

| configuration | CV accuracy |
|---|---:|
| current pipeline | **0.996** |
| + cepstral mean normalisation (CMN) | 0.969 |
| raise log floor to 1e-6 | 0.996 |
| raise log floor 1e-6 + CMN | 0.961 |

**Answer: no.** The domains are 99.6 % linearly separable, and the standard
channel-normalisation fix — CMN, which exists precisely to remove a microphone's
transfer function — moves that to 96.9 %. That is a real improvement and
nowhere near enough. Nothing in the feature pipeline closes a gap this wide.

**This is the evidence that justifies asking for real recordings.** It was
measured before the request, not assumed after it.

> A methodological note. The first version of this measurement compared *means*
> and reported that CMN reduced the gap to exactly 0.000. That number was an
> artefact: CMN sets each clip's per-coefficient mean to zero by construction, so
> a mean-based distance is necessarily zero and measures nothing. The separability
> metric above replaced it. Recorded because CLAUDE.md rule 7 says a surprisingly
> good result should make you suspect the evaluation first, and here it did.

## 2. How big is the shift, and where does it live?

Cohen's *d* per MFCC coefficient, real minus synthetic. `|d| > 0.8` is a large
shift; `|d| > 2` means the distributions barely overlap.

| | mean \|d\| | largest |
|---|---:|---|
| all frames | 0.25 | — |
| **speech frames only** | **0.38** | c2, d = −0.81 |

Displacement of real speech in the model's normalised input space: **0.48 SD**.

This is the part that surprises. The per-coefficient shift is *modest* — mostly
"small" by conventional effect-size standards — yet the model's output collapses
from 0.94 to 0.10. A small shift producing a total collapse means **the decision
surface has very little margin on real audio**. The model is not confidently
wrong about real speech; it is barely holding on, and any context change tips it.

Spectral tilt between the domains is negligible (**−0.12**), which is why a
channel model like `mic_band_limit` cannot fix this: there is no simple filter
to undo.

## 3. The mechanism: real audio saturates the mel log floor

| | mel bins at or below `LOG_FLOOR` | median mel energy |
|---|---:|---:|
| synthetic TTS | 8.27 % | 8.9e-03 |
| **real INMP441** | **20.74 %** | 9.5e-02 |

Real clips clamp **2.5× more often**. A clamped bin returns a constant
(`log(1e-10) = −23.03`) regardless of what was there, and the DCT smears that
constant across every cepstral coefficient. Real audio has *higher* median mel
energy but far more bins pinned at the floor — high energy at low frequencies,
very little in the upper speech bands where `/SH/` lives.

That is consistent with the recording-level problem already measured in EXP-006:
the speech band sits at −25 to −34 dBFS, and the INMP441's sub-100 Hz rumble
carries most of the broadband energy. Measured word-to-room-tone SNR across the
18 usable recordings: **median 3.6 dB broadband** (min −3.7, max 12.9), against
12–20 dB in the speech band.

### The noise result, and what it does and does not mean

Adding **broadband** noise to real audio raises its score dramatically:

| | median P(keyword) |
|---|---:|
| real positives, clean | 0.065 |
| real positives, with the factory's noise | **0.768** |
| synthetic positives, clean | 0.943 |
| synthetic positives, with noise | 0.943 |

Adding **low-frequency** noise does nothing — it sits below the 125 Hz mel floor
and never reaches the features. An earlier version of this test used pink noise
generated by `cumsum` and concluded noise did not help; it was measuring energy
the pipeline discards.

Critically, **noise does not manufacture detections from nothing**: adding white
noise to 200 speech negatives left the median at 0.0004 and produced 0 of 200
above threshold. The model has *not* learned "noise ⇒ keyword".

What the result means is narrower and more useful: broadband energy lifts real
audio's clamped mel bins back into the range the model was trained on. It is
evidence for the log-floor mechanism, **not** a fix — the device will hear a
quiet room, and deliberately adding noise to make numbers improve would be
tuning the measurement rather than the model.

## 4. A second, independent shift: the test split is the only clean split

| split | positives (synth) | positives (real) | near_homophone | hard_negative | speech_negative | background |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| train | 74.6 % | — | 74.7 % | 74.7 % | 74.7 % | 75.2 % |
| validation | 77.8 % | 81.5 % | 73.9 % | 74.1 % | 74.2 % | — |
| **test** | — | **0.0 %** | **0.0 %** | **0.0 %** | **0.0 %** | — |

*(percentage of clips carrying additive noise augmentation)*

Augmentation is applied **symmetrically across classes** — there is no
positive-versus-negative confound, and an earlier claim in this session that
there was one came from a broken counting expression and is withdrawn.

The real asymmetry is by **split**: `augment()` returns early for test
(`if split == "test": return x, meta`), so the whole test set is clean while
training is 75 % noisy. That is a deliberate "keep test pristine" choice, and it
is wrong in a specific way — **"clean" is not neutral, it is its own
distribution, and one the device never encounters.** A real deployment window
contains room tone.

Combined with §3, this explains the gap between the two headline numbers:
validation real recall 20.4 % (81 % of those clips noisy) versus test real
recall 0 % (0 % noisy).

## 5. Duration is not the problem

| | median | p10 | p90 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| synthetic | 700 ms | 570 | 836 |
| real | 630 ms | 350 | 760 |

Comparable, and both inside the 250–880 ms admissible band. Ruled out.

## 6. Conclusions

1. **Real training audio is necessary.** 99.6 % separability, unmoved by CMN or
   log-floor changes, is not a preprocessing problem. This justifies D-015.
2. **The model has thin margin, not a wrong concept.** A 0.48 SD input
   displacement should not cause a total collapse. Real positives in training
   should therefore help disproportionately — the decision surface needs
   anchoring in the real domain, not rebuilding.
3. **The architecture is not implicated.** Nothing measured here points at model
   capacity or structure, so per the session brief the architecture stays fixed.
4. **The test split must become realistic, not pristine.** Test clips should
   carry the recording's own room tone rather than digital silence. This changes
   a number in the favourable direction, so it is declared here in advance, with
   its justification, and **the primary metric remains streaming detection over
   raw unmodified recordings** — which no clip-construction choice can flatter.
5. **Recording level is the binding data-quality constraint.** 20.7 % mel-floor
   clamping traces directly to a weak speech band. The corrected recorder now
   gates on this at capture time.

## 6b. Postscript — the finding this analysis did NOT predict

Everything above is about the *feature* domain gap, and it correctly concluded
that real training data was required. It missed a second, larger effect that only
appeared once model-2 existed: a **16 dB train/deploy level mismatch**. The
factory peak-normalises positives to -6 dBFS; the streaming path fed raw audio at
-18.7 dBFS. model-2 scored real *clips* at 100% recall and real *streaming* at
0/15 until the inference path normalised window level, at which point it reached
14/15.

Both changes were necessary. Real training data with raw streaming: 0/15. Level
normalisation without real training data: 0/15. See `MODEL_COMPARISON.md` and
D-016.

The general lesson is the one already in CLAUDE.md rule 2, arriving from a new
direction: a clip metric and a streaming metric are different measurements, and
here they disagreed by the entire result.

---

## 7. What this does not prove

* Separability being high does not prove real data *will* fix it — it proves
  preprocessing will not. The retraining experiment is the test.
* All real audio is one speaker, one microphone, one room. Nothing here is
  speaker-independent, and a domain gap measured on one voice may differ for
  another.
* The linear-separability probe uses a linear classifier on summary statistics;
  a different probe could give a different number. The conclusion rests on the
  size of the effect (0.996), not its third decimal.
* The log-floor mechanism is supported by the clamping rates and the broadband
  noise response. It has not been proven causal by ablating the floor and
  retraining.
